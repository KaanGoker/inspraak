About Egmont

Goals
The Royal Institute for International Relations is an independent think-tank based in Brussels. Its interdisciplinary research is conducted in a spirit of total academic freedom. Drawing on the expertise of its own research fellows, as well as that of external specialists, both Belgian and foreign, it provides analysis and policy options that are meant to be as operational as possible. Along with research and meetings, the Institute has also developed specialised training activities, both in Brussels and abroad. It can, on request, offer specific programmes for visiting and resident diplomats and foreign professionals. Close cooperation with other research centres, both in Belgium, in Europe and beyond, has resulted in a growing number of joint conferences and in more structured cooperations on research and publications. This has proved to be mutually beneficial and enriching.

In 2024, the Egmont Institute pursued influencing activities directed at EU institutions with explicit reference to concrete legislative proposals,...In 2024, the Egmont Institute pursued influencing activities directed at EU institutions with explicit reference to concrete legislative proposals, regulations, and strategic instruments. One example is Egmont’s contributions around the EU Joint Communication on the Climate-Security Nexus, using a policy brief and related workshops to assess its implementation one year after adoption, thereby engaging Commission & EEAS actors with feedback on this communication. Egmont also engaged directly with instruments such as the Digital Services Act and the Digital Markets Act. Staff research, publications, and events address how these regulations are shaping the digital economy, platform governance, competition policy, and dissemination of responsibilities between digital platforms, users and regulators. Examples include foresight workshops under Belgium’s Council Presidency to feed into the next European Commission’s mandate and the EU Strategic Agenda 2024-2029: Egmont convened expert workshops on “Charting a Green and Competitive Future for Europe,” which addressed green transition measures, industrial policy instruments, and regulatory levers for strengthening Europe’s strategic autonomy. These discussions were aimed explicitly to influence the policy content of future legislative or regulatory frameworks concerning the European Green Deal, EU competitiveness instruments, and industrial policy. Egmont also ran a series called Promoting European Democracy and Solidarity in a Wider EU, which involved foresight workshops targeting the rule of law, EU enlargement, fiscal policy coordination (including instruments under the Stability and Growth Pact, or EU budgetary governance), and the legal frameworks that undergird democracy and solidarity in EU governance. These exercises aimed to influence EU institutional thinking around the European Commission proposals and Council conclusions in those fields. Other influencing activity focused on policymaking in digital and AI regulation. For example, the Institute published on the subject of the EU AI Act, particularly in its external relations and dimension vis-à-vis partner countries, proposing how Europe might help shape responsible AI adoption abroad and how the AI Act’s regulatory framework can engage with digital ecosystems in Africa. Likewise, publications and commentaries addressed the Capital Markets Union (CMU) as a financial, economic and strategic instrument, arguing that completing the CMU is necessary to support defence capabilities, technological innovation (such as quantum, AI, space systems), and Europe’s strategic autonomy. Through events and input papers, Egmont also addressed EU policy instruments like the Conference on the Future of Europe, structure of the European Parliament’s legislative proposals, instruments for enlargement policy, rule of law conditionality mechanisms, and the European Democracy Action Plan / European Democracy Package (sometimes referenced as the “European Democracy Shield”). Egmont’s workshops often sought to generate recommendations for these proposals or for future modifications in light of democratic resilience, countering disinformation, strengthening civic participation. Egmont’s influencing in 2024 was not only in general policy areas, but repeatedly targeted: • The legislative frameworks of the Digital Services Act and Digital Markets Act • The EU Joint Communication on the Climate-Security Nexus • The European AI Act and associated regulatory tools for AI, including its impact beyond EU borders • Capital Markets Union (CMU) as an instrument of financial policy and strategic autonomy • EU instruments for enlargement, rule of law conditionality, and democracy governance (through, for example, legal and budgetary instruments under EU treaties or EU legislative proposals).

What the Commission proposes

The European Commission is conducting a review of the scope, functioning, and efficiency of Regulation (EU) 2022/1031 on the International Procurement Instrument (IPI). The review evaluates the implementation of the regulation, including investigation processes, consultations, thresholds, exceptions, and the first adopted IPI measure regarding medical devices from China. It aims to assess the instrument's effectiveness in tackling procurement barriers in non-EU countries and ensuring a level playing field for EU operators. The findings will be presented in a report to the European Parliament and the Council by August 2027.

  • Review of the scope, functioning, and efficiency of the IPI Regulation pursuant to Article 14.
  • Assessment of investigation procedures, third-country consultations, thresholds, and types of measures.
  • Evaluation of the application and implementation of the first IPI measure concerning Chinese medical devices.
  • Analysis of the efficiency of online complaint tools and rules for determining origin.
  • Execution of a 12-week online public consultation through the Have your say portal.
  • Publication of a factual summary of consultation results and an implementation report to the European Parliament and the Council.

What is asked: Respondents are asked to provide views and evidence on the implementation, scope, functioning, and efficiency of the IPI Regulation in tackling non-EU procurement barriers, achieving market openness, and ensuring a level playing field, as well as the application of specific measures adopted under the Regulation.

What the Commission proposes

The European Commission has proposed a new regulation on the European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation (Europol) to reinforce its operational role in combating serious cross-border crime and terrorism. The initiative replaces Regulation (EU) 2016/794, updates Europol's mandate to address a changing security landscape, and provides for increased resources and staff. It also establishes structured cooperation between Europol and eu-LISA for information management and ICT services while ensuring full alignment with EU data protection frameworks.

  • Replaces Regulation (EU) 2016/794 to establish a revised mandate for Europol.
  • Aims to enhance Europol's operational capabilities against online and offline serious crime and terrorism.
  • Establishes structured cooperation between Europol and eu-LISA for information systems and communication infrastructure.
  • Allocates an estimated EUR 1.053 billion and 910 additional staff posts over the 2028-2034 MFF.
  • Maintains alignment with the EU data protection framework and fundamental rights standards.

What the Commission proposes

The European Commission proposes a new regulation on the European Union Agency for Criminal Justice Cooperation (Eurojust) to repeal and replace Regulation (EU) 2018/1727. This initiative responds to an evaluation showing that while Eurojust is indispensable, its operations need greater efficiency and a wider mandate to tackle modern cross-border crime. The proposal expands Eurojust's competence into emerging areas such as cybercrime, gender-based violence, and violations of EU restrictive measures, while transforming it into a more proactive analytical hub. It also aims to streamline the agency's internal structure and improve cooperation with partners like Europol and the European Public Prosecutor Office.

  • Repeals and replaces Regulation (EU) 2018/1727 to establish a renewed legal framework for Eurojust.
  • Expands Eurojust's mandate to cover emerging crime areas including cybercrime, gender-based violence, and violations of EU restrictive measures.
  • Transforms Eurojust from a reactive coordination body into a strategic and analytical hub for cross-border judicial cooperation.
  • Standardises National Members' status and powers to improve governance and operational efficiency.
  • Addresses findings from the 2025 evaluation regarding complex decision-making, wide case scope, and slow procedures with third countries.
  • Aligns with related revisions of the Europol Regulation, the European Investigation Order Directive, and the European Union Data Protection Regulation.

What is asked: The Commission sought the views of citizens, Member States, and other relevant stakeholders on Eurojust and the Eurojust Regulation via an online public consultation to inform the impact assessment for the revision of the Eurojust Regulation.

Revision of EU rules on public procurement until 30 Nov 2026 possible fit Public procurement is a horizontal EU rules file, and its preferences for strategic supply chains touch Egmont's European institutional and security interests only peripherally.

Full consultation page Answer on the Commission's site

What the Commission proposes

The European Commission proposes a revision of EU public procurement rules through a new regulation that repeals the existing 2014 directives on public procurement, utilities, and concessions. The initiative aims to modernize and simplify rules, reduce administrative burdens, and enable preferences for European products in strategic sectors to secure supply chains and add EU value. It also amends multiple sector-specific regulations and directives containing procurement provisions to improve coherence and transparency. Furthermore, the proposal addresses high administrative burdens and legal uncertainty identified in previous evaluations regarding public buyers and businesses.

  • Revises and replaces Directives 2014/23/EU, 2014/24/EU, and 2014/25/EU with a single Public Procurement Act regulation.
  • Enables preference for European products in strategic sectors to ensure security of supply and EU added value.
  • Aims to modernize and simplify rules, specifically considering EU start-ups and innovators.
  • Amends various sector-specific regulations and directives that contain public procurement provisions.
  • Addresses administrative burdens and legal uncertainty that currently affect public buyers and businesses, including SMEs.

What the Commission proposes

The European Commission proposes the Cloud and AI Development Act to establish a framework of measures aimed at strengthening Europe's cloud and artificial intelligence ecosystem. The initiative focuses on three pillars, which include research and innovation for greening infrastructure, industrial investments to triple EU data processing capacity, and actions to increase secure data processing capacities for cloud providers in the EU. Ultimately, the proposal seeks to address infrastructure gaps, reduce critical dependencies on non-European providers, and enhance the Union's digital sovereignty and competitiveness.

  • Addresses the EU gap in cloud and AI infrastructure capacity through three distinct pillars.
  • Promotes research and innovation to accelerate sustainable cloud and AI computing technologies.
  • Aims to triple the EU data processing capacity over the next 5 to 7 years via industrial investments.
  • Introduces measures to increase secure data processing capacities for high-critical use cases.
  • Establishes common criteria for sovereign cloud computing services to reduce extraterritorial dependencies.
  • Promotes open-source solutions and joint public procurement activities across the public sector.
